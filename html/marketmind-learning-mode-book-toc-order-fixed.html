ANALYST DIAGNOSTICS

24 “Hidden Patterns” Worth Investigating

These are not buy/sell secrets. They are cross-checks that reveal where reported numbers, cash flow, positioning or expectations may be telling different stories.

01

Profit rises, operating cash falls

Reported earnings improve while operating cash flow deteriorates. Investigate receivables, inventory, contract assets, non-cash gains and working-capital timing. One year can be normal; a persistent multi-year gap matters more.

Analyst use: Quality-of-earnings warning, not proof of manipulation.
02

Sales rise, receivables rise much faster

Customers may be taking longer to pay, credit terms may have loosened, mix may have shifted, or revenue recognition may deserve closer review. Calculate receivable days and aging.

Analyst use: Ask why cash collection is not keeping pace with sales.
03

Sales rise, inventory rises much faster

Possible pre-build for demand, supply-chain buffering, acquisitions, slow-moving stock or weakening sell-through. Pair inventory days with gross margin and discounting commentary.

Analyst use: Inventory growth is a question generator, not an automatic bearish signal.
04

CFO jumps because payables jump

Cash flow can improve when a company delays supplier payments. This is different from cash improving because customers pay faster or margins improve.

Analyst use: Separate sustainable operating cash from temporary working-capital release.
05

EPS rises mainly from other income

Headline profit can grow while core operating profit is flat. Inspect interest income, asset sales, fair-value gains and exceptional items.

Analyst use: Normalize earnings before applying a valuation multiple.
06

EBITDA rises while capex/CWIP explodes

Growth investment may be legitimate, but large capital commitments can convert attractive accounting profit into weak free cash flow and execution risk.

Analyst use: Compare project returns, commissioning schedule and funding.
07

ROE rises while leverage rises faster

Higher debt can mechanically amplify equity returns. Use DuPont analysis and interest coverage before labeling the business more efficient.

Analyst use: High ROE is strongest when supported by operating economics, not only leverage.
08

EPS rises while share count also rises

Acquisitions, stock compensation or repeated issuance can grow total profit while diluting per-share ownership.

Analyst use: Track diluted shares and free cash flow per share.
09

Exceptional costs appear every year

A supposedly one-off restructuring, impairment or adjustment that repeats deserves normalization and skepticism about “adjusted” profit.

Analyst use: Recurring exceptions may be part of normal economics.
10

Promoter pledge rises

Pledged shares can create financing and forced-selling risk if collateral values fall. Context, purpose and trend matter.

Analyst use: Treat rising pledge as a governance/financial-risk checkpoint.
11

Auditor changes alongside accounting shifts

Auditor changes can be routine, but when combined with policy changes, qualifications, delayed filings or governance concerns they deserve deeper reading.

Analyst use: Use filings and auditor disclosures; avoid unsupported accusations.
12

Great results, stock falls

The result may be below expectations, guidance may weaken, valuation may be stretched, or investors may take profit after a pre-result rally.

Analyst use: Price reacts to expectation changes, not headline sign.
13

Bad results, stock rises

The outcome may be less bad than feared, forward guidance may improve, or positioning may be extremely negative.

Analyst use: Compare actual outcome with the market-implied scenario.
14

Breakout occurs on weak volume

Price can still trend, but participation provides less confirmation. Watch follow-through, retest behavior and sector strength.

Analyst use: Do not label every low-volume breakout false; demand confirmation.
15

Huge volume, little price progress

High effort with limited result can indicate absorption or strong two-sided conflict. Direction depends on location and subsequent behavior.

Analyst use: A diagnostic clue, never a standalone reversal signal.
16

Price makes new high, RSI makes lower high

Momentum divergence can warn that the latest advance has less momentum. Strong trends can ignore divergence for long periods.

Analyst use: Require structure or price confirmation before acting.
17

Index makes new high, breadth weakens

A small group of large weights may be carrying the index while broader participation deteriorates.

Analyst use: Track advance-decline, equal-weight and sector breadth.
18

Stock rises, sector rises much faster

Absolute gain can hide relative weakness. The company may be lagging peers despite a green chart.

Analyst use: Compare stock/sector and sector/index ratios.
19

Low P/E occurs at peak margins

Cyclical businesses often look cheapest near peak earnings because the denominator is temporarily elevated.

Analyst use: Normalize mid-cycle earnings before calling it value.
20

High P/E with rising earnings revisions

A high multiple can coexist with strong fundamentals when earnings expectations are being upgraded faster than price.

Analyst use: Valuation must be read with growth quality and revisions.
21

Dividend rises while debt rises

A larger payout may look shareholder-friendly but can be financially weak if funded by borrowing rather than sustainable free cash flow.

Analyst use: Compare dividend with FCF and leverage.
22

Acquisitions drive growth, organic growth stays weak

Reported revenue can expand while the legacy business stagnates. Acquired earnings may come with goodwill, debt and integration risk.

Analyst use: Separate organic and inorganic growth.
23

Margins improve while provisions fall

Lower warranty, credit-loss or other provisions can lift profit. The change may be justified, but assumptions should be compared with history and actual losses.

Analyst use: Check whether reserve releases are repeatable.
24

Price rises into event, IV collapses afterward

In options, a correct directional move can still lose money after an event because implied volatility falls and time value disappears.

Analyst use: Direction, magnitude, volatility and timing all matter.

Market Glossary

Fast definitions for the vocabulary used throughout the masterbook.

Alpha

Return beyond an appropriate benchmark or risk model; its interpretation depends on the benchmark and period.

Beta

Sensitivity of an asset return to a benchmark; not a complete measure of business risk.

Bid

Highest displayed price a buyer is currently willing to pay.

Ask

Lowest displayed price a seller is currently willing to accept.

Spread

Difference between bid and ask; one component of execution cost.

Liquidity

Ability to transact meaningful size with limited price impact.

Slippage

Difference between intended/reference price and actual execution price.

Market Cap

Share price multiplied by shares outstanding.

Free Float

Shares realistically available for public trading, excluding strategic/locked holdings as defined by methodology.

Enterprise Value

Value of the operating enterprise across capital providers, commonly equity value plus debt minus cash with adjustments.

EPS

Earnings attributable to common shareholders divided by weighted-average shares.

EBITDA

Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization; an operating proxy, not cash flow.

EBIT

Operating earnings after depreciation/amortization, before financing and taxes.

CFO

Cash flow from operating activities.

FCF

Cash remaining after relevant operating and capital investment; exact definition should be stated.

ROE

Net income relative to average shareholders equity.

ROCE

Operating return relative to capital employed; definitions vary.

ROIC

After-tax operating profit relative to invested operating capital.

NOPAT

Net operating profit after tax, used in ROIC/DCF frameworks.

Gross Margin

Gross profit as a percentage of revenue.

Operating Margin

Operating profit as a percentage of revenue.

Net Margin

Net profit as a percentage of revenue.

DSO

Days Sales Outstanding; approximate time receivables remain uncollected.

DIO

Days Inventory Outstanding; approximate inventory holding period.

DPO

Days Payables Outstanding; approximate supplier-payment period.

CCC

Cash Conversion Cycle = DSO + DIO - DPO.

P/E

Price divided by earnings per share.

P/B

Price divided by book value per share.

EV/EBITDA

Enterprise value divided by EBITDA.

PEG

P/E divided by an earnings-growth rate; a shorthand with major limitations.

DCF

Discounted cash flow valuation.

WACC

Weighted average cost of capital used for appropriately defined enterprise cash flows.

Terminal Value

Estimated value of cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period in a DCF.

Margin of Safety

Buffer between estimated value and purchase price to reflect uncertainty.

Support

Price zone where buying previously slowed or reversed declines.

Resistance

Price zone where selling previously slowed or reversed advances.

Breakout

Move beyond a prior structure/zone that may signal a new price regime if confirmed.

RSI

Relative Strength Index momentum oscillator, commonly scaled 0-100.

MACD

Moving Average Convergence Divergence trend/momentum indicator.

ATR

Average True Range, a measure of typical price range/volatility.

Bollinger Bands

Moving-average envelope commonly based on standard deviation.

Relative Strength

Performance of a security relative to a chosen benchmark; distinct from RSI.

Breadth

Degree of participation across securities in a market move.

Volatility

Dispersion or variability of returns.

Correlation

Degree to which two return series move together, from -1 to +1.

Drawdown

Decline from a previous portfolio or asset peak.

CAGR

Compound annual growth rate between beginning and ending values.

XIRR

Money-weighted annualized return for irregular dated cash flows.

Futures

Standardized derivative contract creating leveraged exposure to an underlying.

Option

Contract giving the buyer a right under defined terms while imposing an obligation on the seller.

Call

Option whose intrinsic value increases when spot exceeds strike.

Put

Option whose intrinsic value increases when strike exceeds spot.

Delta

Approximate option sensitivity to a small move in underlying price.

Gamma

Approximate rate of change of delta with underlying price.

Theta

Approximate option sensitivity to passage of time.

Vega

Approximate option sensitivity to implied volatility.

Implied Volatility

Volatility input consistent with observed option prices under a pricing model.

Open Interest

Number of outstanding derivative contracts that remain open.

Moat

Durable economic mechanism protecting attractive returns from competition.

Pricing Power

Ability to raise price without unacceptable loss of demand.

Operating Leverage

Sensitivity of operating profit to revenue when fixed costs are significant.

Accrual

Accounting recognition not matched by cash movement in the same period.

Dilution

Reduction in existing owners percentage/per-share claim due to new shares or convertible claims.

Buyback

Company repurchase of its own shares; value impact depends on price, funding and retirement mechanics.

CHAPTER 01MODULE 01Foundation

What the Stock Market Actually Is

A stock market is a regulated auction where ownership claims in listed companies are continuously repriced by buyers and sellers. Price is not the same thing as value: price is the latest clearing point between demand and supply, while value is an estimate of future cash flows and risk.

Why this matters

Beginners often treat the ticker as the business. Professionals reverse that order: first understand the business and its economics, then ask whether the market price offers a reasonable return for the risk.

FORMULA / FRAMEWORK
Market price = expectations about future cash flows + discount rate + positioning + liquidity + narrative. A useful mental model is: Business reality -> expectations -> orders -> price.
WORKED EXAMPLE

Imagine a company earning Rs 10 per share. If investors expect profits to grow 20% for years, they may pay Rs 300. If growth expectations fall to 8%, the business can still be profitable while the share price falls sharply because the expectation embedded in the old price changed.

CHAPTER 02MODULE 01Instruments

Shares, Bonds, ETFs, REITs & Derivatives

Financial instruments represent different legal and economic claims. Equity gives residual ownership, bonds are contractual debt claims, ETFs pool assets, REITs distribute income from real-estate portfolios, and derivatives derive value from an underlying asset or index.

Why this matters

Choosing the wrong instrument can create a risk profile very different from the idea. The same bullish view expressed through cash equity, futures, or short-dated calls can have radically different downside, leverage and time-decay behavior.

FORMULA / FRAMEWORK
Equity return ~= price appreciation + dividends. Bond return depends on coupon, yield changes and credit risk. Derivative payoff depends on contract terms, leverage, time and volatility.
WORKED EXAMPLE

If you expect an index to rise 5% over six months, buying an index ETF exposes you roughly one-for-one. A futures position may require only a fraction of notional as margin, magnifying gains and losses. An option can cap downside at premium but may lose value even if direction is right when timing or volatility is wrong.

CHAPTER 03MODULE 01Market Microstructure

Orders, Bid-Ask, Liquidity & Slippage

Every trade crosses or joins an order book. The best bid is the highest visible buying price; the best ask is the lowest visible selling price. Their difference is the bid-ask spread. Liquidity describes how easily meaningful size can trade without moving price too much.

Why this matters

A strategy that looks profitable on a chart can fail after spreads, slippage and market impact. Execution quality matters especially in small-cap stocks, event-driven trades and large orders.

FORMULA / FRAMEWORK
Approximate execution cost = spread cost + slippage + fees + market impact. Limit orders control price but not execution; market orders prioritize execution but not price.
WORKED EXAMPLE

Suppose a stock shows bid Rs 100.00 and ask Rs 100.30. A market buy of 10 shares may fill near Rs 100.30, but an order of 50,000 shares could consume several ask levels and average much higher. The quoted price was not available for the whole order.

CHAPTER 04MODULE 01Structure

Market Cap, Free Float & Indices

Market capitalization is the market value of all outstanding equity. Free float narrows the focus to shares realistically available for public trading. Indices use transparent rules to represent a segment of the market and may weight constituents by free-float market cap.

Why this matters

Index inclusion can influence passive flows, but size is not quality. A large market cap may reflect scale, high valuation, or both. Free float also affects liquidity and the amount passive funds need to trade.

FORMULA / FRAMEWORK
Market Cap = Price x Shares Outstanding. Free-Float Market Cap = Price x Shares Available to Public Investors.
WORKED EXAMPLE

Company A and B both have Rs 1 lakh crore market cap. If promoters own 75% of A but only 30% of B, B has much more free float. An index using free-float weights can therefore assign B a much larger weight despite equal headline market cap.

CHAPTER 05MODULE 01Corporate Actions

Dividends, Splits, Bonus, Rights & Buybacks

Corporate actions alter cash distribution, share count or ownership mechanics. They can affect quoted price mechanically, but they do not create economic value simply because more shares appear in the account.

Why this matters

Investors frequently mistake cosmetic changes for wealth creation. Understanding adjustment mechanics prevents false conclusions about sudden price drops, EPS changes and historical charts.

FORMULA / FRAMEWORK
After a simple 2-for-1 split, theoretical price halves and share count doubles, leaving market value approximately unchanged before market movement. Dividend ex-date adjustments similarly reflect cash leaving the company.
WORKED EXAMPLE

If you own 100 shares at Rs 1,000 and a 2-for-1 split occurs, you may own 200 shares around Rs 500. Your economic ownership is still roughly Rs 1,00,000. A Rs 20 dividend is different: cash is transferred from company to shareholders, so the stock may adjust around the ex-date.

CHAPTER 06MODULE 01IPO Analysis

How to Analyze an IPO Without Hype

An IPO converts a private ownership story into a public-market security. The key work is not predicting listing-day excitement; it is understanding what is being sold, why capital is being raised, who is exiting, and what valuation assumes about future economics.

Why this matters

IPO marketing naturally emphasizes opportunity. A disciplined process separates the business, the offer structure, the financial history, governance and valuation from the narrative.

FORMULA / FRAMEWORK
IPO checklist = Business quality + use of proceeds + selling shareholders + financial quality + dilution + valuation + risks + post-issue capital structure.
WORKED EXAMPLE

Assume a company issues Rs 1,000 crore of fresh shares and existing shareholders sell Rs 3,000 crore. Only the fresh portion goes to the company. If the prospectus narrative focuses on expansion but most of the offer is an offer-for-sale, ask what the business actually receives.

CHAPTER 07MODULE 02Business Model

Understand the Business Model Before Ratios

A business model explains who pays, what they buy, why they choose the company, how often they buy, what resources are required to serve them, and where profit and cash are generated. Ratios are outputs of this engine.

Why this matters

The same P/E can mean very different things for subscription software, commodity producers, banks or retailers. Without business-model context, ratio comparisons become mechanically misleading.

FORMULA / FRAMEWORK
Revenue engine = Customers x volume/frequency x price. Profit engine = Revenue x unit margin - fixed costs - financing/tax. Cash engine adjusts for working capital and capital expenditure.
WORKED EXAMPLE

A retailer may grow revenue 20% by opening 25% more stores while same-store sales are flat. A software company may grow 20% with little incremental capital because existing infrastructure serves more users. Identical headline growth can therefore have very different quality.

CHAPTER 08MODULE 02Competitive Analysis

Industry Structure, Moats & Competitive Advantage

A moat is not a compliment; it is an economic mechanism that protects attractive returns from competition. Common sources include switching costs, network effects, scale economics, cost advantages, brands, regulation and scarce assets.

Why this matters

High returns attract competitors. Sustainable value creation requires either barriers that protect returns or a market expanding fast enough to absorb competition.

FORMULA / FRAMEWORK
Competitive advantage test: high ROIC relative to cost of capital + durability + evidence of reinvestment opportunities. Porter-style industry forces help explain where bargaining power sits.
WORKED EXAMPLE

A payments network can become more useful as more merchants and users join, creating a network effect. A commodity producer may have no brand moat but can still possess a structural cost advantage if its mines sit at the low end of the global cost curve.

CHAPTER 09MODULE 02Economics

Unit Economics & Operating Leverage

Unit economics decomposes a business into the profitability of one customer, order, store, room, seat, loan or other operating unit. Operating leverage describes how profit changes when revenue grows faster than fixed costs.

Why this matters

CHAPTER 58MODULE 01Market Microstructure

Algorithmic Trading & High-Frequency Trading (HFT)

Algorithmic trading uses computer rules to generate, route or execute orders. High-frequency trading is a specialized subset built around very low latency, high message rates and short holding periods. HFT is part of market microstructure; it is not a magical indicator visible from a retail chart.

Why this matters

Understanding HFT helps investors distinguish business analysis from execution mechanics. Algorithms can affect spreads, queue position and very short-term price formation, but they do not remove fundamental uncertainty or create a guaranteed way to predict the next move.

FORMULA / FRAMEWORK
Conceptual HFT loop: Market data -> signal/quote logic -> risk checks -> order placement/cancellation -> execution -> inventory/risk update, often repeated at machine speed.
WORKED EXAMPLE

A market-making algorithm may continuously quote a bid and ask, earning small spreads while managing inventory risk. If volatility suddenly jumps, it can widen quotes or reduce size. A long-term investor sees the resulting spread change but should not mistake it for a fundamental change in the company.

Short selling expresses a view that a security may fall, typically by borrowing shares under permitted market mechanisms and later repurchasing them. FORMULA / FRAMEWORK

Why this matters

Large institutions often split orders to reduce market impact. This can create persistent intraday demand or supply, but identifying it from public data is probabilistic and difficult.

FORMULA / FRAMEWORK
